# 2. Ships and Their Profiles

Every ship in the game is a playing piece of its own, with a profile that says how fast it is, how
hard it is to hurt and what it carries. This chapter explains the profile, the kinds of ship, the
base each ship stands on, and the Resolve that measures its captain and crew.

## Kinds of ship

There are three kinds of warship:

- **Battleships** are the largest. They take enormous punishment and carry the heaviest guns, but
  they are slow to turn and need lighter ships around them.
- **Cruisers** are the backbone of a fleet: well armed, quick enough, and able to operate alone for
  long stretches.
- **Escorts** are the small ships: frigates, destroyers and the like. They are fast and lightly
  armed, and they fight in squadrons (chapter 11).

Battleships and cruisers together are called **capital ships**. Anything smaller is an escort.

Some rules also speak of **defences**: fixed stations and other things that cannot manoeuvre.
Defences are the easiest target on the gunnery table (chapter 5), and a ship that barely moves is
shot at as if it were one.

## The profile

Here is the profile of a Freemen Lantern class cruiser, as an example:

| Type | Hits | Speed | Turns | Armour | Shields | Turrets |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cruiser | 8 | 20 cm | 45° | 6+ front, 5+ elsewhere | 2 | 2 |

| Weapon | Range or speed | Firepower or Strength | Fire arc |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lance, port | 30 cm | 2 | left |
| Lance, starboard | 30 cm | 2 | right |
| Weapons battery, port | 30 cm | 6 | left |
| Weapons battery, starboard | 30 cm | 6 | right |
| Torpedoes, prow | 30 cm | 6 | front |

What each entry means:

- **Name and class.** Every capital ship deserves a name of its own; escorts can do without. Its
  class tells you which profile it uses. Ships of one class share a hull, but two classes of the
  same size can differ a great deal.
- **Type and Hits.** The type is battleship, cruiser or escort. **Hits** is how much damage the hull
  and crew can take before the ship is a wreck. This book also calls them **damage points**; the
  two words mean the same. A ship's **starting** hits are the number on its profile, whatever it
  has lost since.
- **Speed.** How far the ship moves in one Manoeuvre Phase. Special orders and damage can raise or
  lower it (chapters 4 and 7).
- **Turns.** How sharply the ship can turn when it turns: 45° or 90°.
- **Shields.** How many hits its shields can stop before they are overwhelmed (chapter 5).
- **Armour.** The score an attacker must roll, equal or better, on a D6 to hurt it with most
  weapons. Some ships are tougher from one side, often the prow; the profile then gives a front
  value and a value for the rest.
- **Turrets.** The small, fast guns spread along the hull to shoot down torpedoes and attack craft
  in their last moments (chapter 6).
- **Armament.** The ship's main weapons and where they are mounted. For each one:
  - **Range or speed.** How far a gun reaches, in cm. For ordnance that flies to its target, such
    as torpedoes and attack craft, this is the distance it moves each Ordnance Phase instead.
  - **Firepower or Strength.** How much the weapon puts out: higher is better. Weapons batteries
    have a firepower; lances, torpedoes and other special weapons have a Strength. For launch
    bays it is the number of attack craft squadrons launched at once.
  - **Fire arc.** Which arc or arcs it can fire into (chapter 1). Broadside weapons cover the left
    or right arc. Dorsal mounts along the top of the hull usually cover left, front and right. Few
    ships carry guns aimed to the rear, where the drive's heat blinds the gunners. A weapon that
    fires "all round" covers every arc.

## Bases

There are two sizes of flying base:

| Base | Diameter |
|---|---|
| small | 32 mm |
| large | 60 mm |

- A ship or defence with **3 or more shields, or more than 10 hits,** must use a large base.
- Any capital ship may choose a large base. If it does, it counts as carrying tractor fields at no
  cost. Tractor fields do nothing except what the bigger base does on its own: it is easier for
  the ship to ram or to board, and it is a bigger target for enemy ordnance.

## Resolve

A ship's **Resolve (Rs)** measures how experienced its crew is and how quick its captain is in
battle. The higher, the better. Resolve matters most when a ship tries to go on special orders.

### Starting Resolve

Before a one-off battle, roll a D6 for each ship's Resolve:

| D6 | Crew | Resolve |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Untried | 6 |
| 2-3 | Experienced | 7 |
| 4-5 | Veteran | 8 |
| 6 | Crack | 9 |

- Each capital ship rolls on its own, even when it is in a squadron.
- Each escort squadron rolls once, and every ship in it uses that one value.
- A fleet's own rules may give it a different table, or fixed values.

### Resolve Tests

To take a **Resolve Test**, roll 2D6 (or the number of dice the rule names) and compare the total
with the ship's Resolve. A total equal to or below the Resolve passes. Anything higher fails.

- Resolve can never be raised above 10.
- A 2D6 roll of 11 or 12 always fails, whatever the Resolve, unless a rule says plainly that it
  does not.
- Some tests use more or fewer dice: ramming a smaller ship rolls 3D6, for example (chapter 8).
  The more dice, the harder the test.

The Resolve Test a ship takes to go on special orders is called a **Command check**; it has
modifiers of its own (chapter 4). Every other Resolve Test, for ramming, for firing past the
nearest enemy, for crossing an asteroid field or for disengaging, is taken without those
modifiers unless its own rule gives some. A ship may take these other tests even after a Command
check has failed earlier in the turn.
